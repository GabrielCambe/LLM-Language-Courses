# 📚 Reading guide: from picture books to novels

Reading is the input you control best: you set the speed, you can re-read, and you *see* the
spelling that French pronunciation hides.

## Why read (the research)
- **Extensive reading works.** A meta-analysis of 49 studies (Jeon & Day, 2016) found that reading
  lots of easy, enjoyable text improves reading proficiency, with **bigger effects for adults and
  for longer programmes**.
- **Graded readers teach vocabulary.** Learners who read *and listened to* 10 graded readers
  learned a large share of the unknown words they met, and kept much of it three months later
  (Webb & Chang, 2015).
- **The 98% rule.** Reading is comfortable, and new words can be guessed from context, when you
  know about **98% of the words** (Nation, 2006).
  👉 **Practical test:** a typical book page has ~250 words, and 2% of 250 = 5. **More than ~5
  unknown words per page = too hard for pleasure reading.** Go down a level; no shame in it.

## How to read (the method)

| Mode | What | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Extensive** (most of your reading) | Easy text, read for the story, **don't look up words**, skip what you don't get | Daily, 10–20 min |
| **Intensive** (a little) | One paragraph, decoded line by line with the **Reading Companion** prompt | 1–2× per week |
| **Read-while-listening** | Read along with the audiobook | When energy is low: the audio carries you |

Rules of thumb:
1. **Enjoyment beats "useful".** A comic you finish is worth more than a classic you abandon.
2. **Look up a word only on its 3rd appearance.** If it keeps coming back, it matters.
3. **Harvest 3–5 chunks per session** into your flashcards (Flashcard Maker prompt → app import).
4. **Daniel Pennac's rights of the reader** (*Comme un roman*, 1992) include the right to skip
   pages, the right *not to finish a book*, and the right to read anything. Use them. Dropping a
   boring book is a strategy, not a failure.

### ⚠️ The "book past tense": passé simple
Novels, fairy tales and history books narrate in the **passé simple**, a past tense used almost
only in writing (*il **parla**, elle **fut**, ils **eurent***). You don't need to *produce* it,
just **recognise** it. Cheat sheet: the *Passé simple* section of [grammar/06-past-future.md](../grammar/06-past-future.md).

## The reading ladder

> 🧭 **Scope guard:** pick **one** book from your current level. Finish it (or drop it), then pick the next.
> Don't buy the whole list.

### 🟢 Level 1 (A1): picture books, comics, kids' news
Short, illustrated, repetitive. The pictures do half the work.

| Title | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ***Petit Ours Brun*** | picture books (toddlers) | Tiny sentences about daily routine |
| ***T'choupi*** | picture books | Very simple, present tense |
| ***Le Loup qui…*** series (Orianne Lallemand) | picture books | Repetitive structure, fun |
| ***Ernest et Célestine*** (Gabrielle Vincent 🇧🇪) | picture books | The originals behind the film (see films guide) |
| ***Les Schtroumpfs*** (Peyo 🇧🇪) | comics | The Smurfs: short dialogues, clear drawings |
| **Graded readers A1**: *Lectures CLE en français facile*, *Lire en français facile* | adapted books | Vocabulary controlled to your level, often with audio |
| ***Le Petit Quotidien*** (ages 6–10) | daily kids' newspaper | Real news in very simple French |

### 🟡 Level 2 (A2): fairy tales, children's classics, comics
| Title | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Perrault's tales, adapted for children**: *Le Petit Chaperon rouge*, *Cendrillon*, *Le Chat botté*, *La Belle au bois dormant* | fairy tales | You already know the stories, so context carries you. Start with modern children's editions. |
| ***La Belle et la Bête*** (Mme Leprince de Beaumont, 1756) | fairy tale | The version everyone adapted, short |
| ***Max et Lili*** (Dominique de Saint Mars) | kids' books | Everyday problems, dialogue-heavy |
| ***Le Petit Nicolas*** (Goscinny & Sempé) | children's classic | Written in a child's voice, funny, spoken style |
| ***Tintin*** (Hergé 🇧🇪), ***Lucky Luke***, ***Gaston Lagaffe*** (Franquin 🇧🇪) | comics | Speech bubbles = spoken French with pictures |
| **Graded readers A2** (same series as above) | adapted classics | Abridged *Les Misérables*, *Les Trois Mousquetaires*… |
| ***Mon Quotidien*** (ages 10–14), **1jour1actu** | kids' news | Current events explained simply |
| **Vikidia** ([vikidia.org](https://fr.vikidia.org)) | encyclopedia | Wikipedia written for 8–13-year-olds: look up any topic you like |
| **RFI *Journal en français facile*** transcripts | news | Read the transcript, then listen |

### 🟠 Level 3 (B1): your first real books
| Title | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ***Le Petit Prince*** (Saint-Exupéry) | novella | Simple sentences, deep ideas; the classic first "real" book |
| **Perrault's original *Contes*** (1697) | fairy tales | Now in 17th-century French: a fun stretch |
| ***Short Stories in French for Beginners*** (Olly Richards) | learner stories | Glossaries and summaries per chapter |
| **Simenon, *Maigret*** novels (🇧🇪) | crime | Famously plain, concrete style; short books |
| **Camus, *L'Étranger*** | novel | Short sentences; narrated mainly in the **passé composé**, which is unusual for a novel |
| **Sagan, *Bonjour tristesse*** | novel | Short, written when she was 18 |
| **Annie Ernaux, *La Place*** | memoir | Very short, deliberately flat style (Nobel Prize 2022) |
| **Faïza Guène, *Kiffe kiffe demain*** | novel | Teen narrator, banlieue slang and verlan |
| **Riad Sattouf, *L'Arabe du futur*** | graphic memoir | Gripping, visual, modern French |
| **Marjane Satrapi, *Persepolis*** | graphic memoir | Originally written in French |
| ***L'Actu*** (14–17), ***Géo Ado***, ***20 minutes*** | news & magazines | Short articles, current topics |

### 🔴 Level 4 (B2): contemporary novels, real press
| Title | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Amélie Nothomb** (🇧🇪): *Stupeur et tremblements*, *Hygiène de l'assassin* | novels | Short, witty, sharp dialogues |
| **Joël Dicker** (🇨🇭): *La Vérité sur l'affaire Harry Quebert* | thriller | Page-turner: momentum carries you |
| **Muriel Barbery**: *L'Élégance du hérisson* | novel | Two narrators, two registers |
| **Fred Vargas**: *Commissaire Adamsberg* series | crime | Quirky characters, rich vocabulary |
| **Daniel Pennac**: *Comme un roman* | essay | About the joy of reading itself (the source of the "rights of the reader") |
| **Leïla Slimani**: *Chanson douce* | novel | Goncourt Prize 2016, short and gripping |
| ***Le Monde***, ***Libération***, ***Le Soir*** (🇧🇪), ***Courrier international*** | press | Choose topics you know, like AI and tech, to boost comprehension |

### 🟣 Level 5 (C1+): classics and long-form
**Maupassant**'s short stories (*Boule de suif*, *Le Horla*), **Dumas**, *Les Trois Mousquetaires*
(the original), **Zola**, **Flaubert**, **Hugo**, *Les Misérables* (unabridged: a marathon),
**La Fontaine**'s *Fables* (verse, archaic), ***Le Monde diplomatique***. Proust when you're feeling heroic.

## 💡 Curiosities
- The term **"fairy tale"** comes from French: Madame d'Aulnoy's collection ***Les Contes des fées***
  (1697) popularised *conte de fées*, which was later translated into English.
- **Belgium is a comics superpower**: Tintin, the Smurfs, Lucky Luke, Gaston and Spirou are all
  Belgian. Brussels has a Comics Art Museum and comic murals painted on buildings around the city.
- Perrault's *Le Petit Chaperon rouge* ends with the wolf eating the girl: no hunter, no rescue.
  The happy ending came later, from the Brothers Grimm.
