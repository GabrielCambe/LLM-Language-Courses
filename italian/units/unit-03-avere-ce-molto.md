# Unit 3: Having and describing: *avere*, *c'è*, *molto* · CEFR A1 · ~7 days

> Good news first: *molto* works exactly like Portuguese *muito*, and *avere* covers most of what
> *ter* does. The one big difference is "there is": Brazilians say *tem*, Italians say ***c'è***.

## 🎯 Can-do goals
- I can say what I have, how old I am, and how I feel (hungry, cold, sleepy…) with *avere*.
- I can say what there is in a place with *c'è / ci sono*.
- I can describe people and things with adjectives that agree.

## 📐 The rule

### *avere* (to have)

| | avere | sounds like | Portuguese |
|---|---|---|---|
| io | **ho** | "o" | tenho |
| tu | **hai** | "ai" | tens / você tem |
| lui / lei / Lei | **ha** | "a" | tem |
| noi | **abbiamo** | "ab-BIA-mo" (bb held) | temos |
| voi | **avete** | "a-VÊ-te" | vocês têm |
| loro | **hanno** | "AN-no" (nn held) | têm |

The h is silent. It's only there to tell *ho* (I have) from *o* (or), *ha* from *a* (to) and
*hanno* from *anno* (year).

### *avere* where Portuguese says *estar com* or *ter*

| Italian | Portuguese |
|---|---|
| *ho fame / ho sete / ho sonno* | estou com fome / sede / sono |
| *ho freddo / ho caldo* | estou com frio / calor (⚠️ *caldo* = hot, not broth) |
| *ho paura* | tenho medo |
| *ho ragione / ho torto* | tenho razão / estou errado |
| *ho bisogno di…* | preciso de… |
| *ho trent'anni* | tenho trinta anos (*anni*: double n!) |

### "There is / there are": *c'è / ci sono* (never *ha*)
- ***c'è*** + singular: *C'è un bar qui vicino.* (Tem um bar aqui perto.)
- ***ci sono*** + plural: *Ci sono tre libri sul tavolo.* (Tem três livros na mesa.)
- Negative: *Non c'è tempo.* *Non ci sono problemi.*
- ❌ *Ha un bar qui* means "**he** has a bar here". Brazilian *tem* and Portuguese/Spanish *há / hay*
  both lead you to the wrong verb. *ci* = "there" (*aí, ali*) + *è / sono*.

### *molto*: exactly like *muito*
- **Before an adjective, or after a verb** → it doesn't change: *molto bello*, *lavoro molto*.
- **Before a noun** → it agrees: *molto tempo, molta acqua, molti amici, molte persone*.

Same as *muito bonito* / *muitos amigos*. ⚡ Quick win (Spanish needs two words, *muy / mucho*;
Italian and Portuguese use one). *troppo* (too much) and *poco* (little) work the same way.

### Adjectives agree, and usually go after the noun

| | singular | plural |
|---|---|---|
| m., -o | un ragazzo **alto** | due ragazzi **alti** |
| f., -o | una ragazza **alta** | due ragazze **alte** |
| m./f., -e | un libro **grande**, una casa **grande** | libri **grandi**, case **grandi** |

A few common ones often go **before** the noun, as in Portuguese: *una bella città, un buon amico,
una grande casa, un piccolo problema, un vecchio amico* (an old = long-time friend).

## ⚠️ Common traps
- ❌ *Ha un problema qui* (for "there's") → ✅ ***C'è** un problema.*
- ❌ *Sono 30 anni* / *Ho 30 ani* → ✅ *Ho 30 **anni**.*
- ❌ *Sono con fame* → ✅ ***Ho** fame.*
- ❌ *molto libri* → ✅ ***molti** libri*: before a noun, *molto* agrees.
- ⚠️ False friends: ***caldo*** = hot (broth = *brodo*); ***bravo*** = good, skilled (angry =
  *arrabbiato*); ***burro*** = butter (donkey = *asino*).
- 🇪🇸 Spanish leak: *hay* → ✅ *c'è*; *muy* → ✅ *molto*; *rojo* → ✅ *rosso*.

## 💡 Curiosity
The Brazilian habit of saying *l* as *u* ("Brasiu") is about 1000 years old. Portuguese already did
it in writing to many Latin words: Latin *multum* → Italian ***molto***, Portuguese *muito*; Latin
*alterum* → Italian ***altro***, Portuguese *outro*. Italian kept the l. So every time you say
*molto* with a real l, you're speaking a bit more like Latin.
